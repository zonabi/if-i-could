# "If I could tell you, I would" — research notes

Compiled 2026-11-24.  Companion to `index.html` (the playable prototype) in this folder.

## Naming

Public sources spell the name **Terence Blanchard** (trumpeter, film and opera composer, SFJAZZ executive artistic director).  Searches for "Terrence Blancher" return nothing relevant.  Confirm the spelling before it goes on anything client-facing.

## Where the technique comes from

- **Roger Dickerson** created it.  He is a New Orleans composer who taught Blanchard as a teenager.  The exercise: write paragraphs using only the words of "If I could tell you, I would", reorder them, then punctuate them (one exclamation mark, two, three, a question mark).  Blanchard describes it as a lesson in phrasing and articulation, taught in words first and in music second.
- Blanchard later found the same ideas in a composition textbook, *The Craft of Musical Composition*.  One interview credits the book to Hindemith, another to Schoenberg.  Hindemith wrote a book with that title; Schoenberg's is *Fundamentals of Musical Composition*.  Ask Terence which he means.
- Blanchard says he teaches the exercise wherever he goes.  A Stage 32 film-scoring class bills it as the key to his composing process, alongside a "5 step process to a composition" that the public listing does not spell out.

## What the exercise teaches

1. **Limited material is enough.**  Seven words produce far more sentences than students expect.  A composition can grow out of its own opening idea.
2. **The shape is the idea.**  Move a theme through each degree of the scale and the intervals change while the contour stays.  Listeners still hear it as the same thought.
3. **Punctuation is articulation.**  `!`, `!!`, `!!!` and `?` are dynamics, accent and inflection.  This is the half of the game most write-ups skip, and the one the "tell! tell!! Tell!!! if?" example shows.
4. **Let the material lead.**  Dickerson's wider advice was to listen for what the music wants to be, and to distrust the ego's urge to bring in something unrelated.

## The musical version (from a student report of his SFJAZZ workshop)

Start with a motif as scale degrees: **6 2 8 3 7** (A D C E B in C).

1. Shift every degree up the scale: 7 3 2 4 8, then 8 4 3 5 9, and so on.
2. Hold the first note and attach each shifted version to it.
3. Take step 2 through every key.
4. Retrograde (play it backwards).
5. Take step 4 through every key.
6. Invert the intervals (up a 3rd becomes down a 3rd).
7. Take step 6 through every key.

Repeating this is meant to make the pathways automatic, so they show up while improvising and solos develop one idea.  The same student noted the downside: so many options that choosing becomes the hard part.  That is a product opportunity.

## The moves, words to music

| Word move | Example | Musical equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Reorder | "would you tell" | Permutation, retrograde |
| Leave out | "if you could" | Fragmentation |
| Repeat | "tell tell tell" | Repetition, sequence |
| Accent | "tell! tell!! Tell!!!" | Dynamics, accent, crescendo |
| Question | "if?" | Rising inflection, unresolved ending |
| Breath | commas, ellipses | Rests, phrase length |
| (music only) | | Diatonic shift, inversion, key change, rhythm change |

## App directions to try

1. **Words are notes** (built in the prototype).  Each word is a pitch.  Building a sentence plays a melody, and punctuation changes how it is played.  Teaches the idea in under a minute with no notation.
2. **Motif lab** (built in the prototype).  The same moves applied to pitch, with a shape row and an interval row so the student sees what stays and what changes.
3. **Record your own motif.**  Sing or play five notes, the app transcribes them and deals them back as tiles.  Makes it the student's material.
4. **The daily sentence.**  A new seven-token set each day (words one day, a motif the next).  Make two, then see what others made.  The reveal of other people's answers is the moment that opened things up in the live session.
5. **Play-along drill.**  The app walks the seven workshop steps through all twelve keys with a backing track and listens to the student's instrument.
6. **Choose and build.**  Addresses the too-many-options problem: pick three variations, order them, and the app strings them into an eight-bar phrase.  This is where "level up your compositions" lives.
7. **Teacher mode.**  Terence (or any teacher) sets the token set and sees a wall of student answers, sorted by which moves each one used.
